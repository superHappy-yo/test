\begin{abstract}
Operator fusion decisions depend on tensor shape, precision, and intermediate traffic.
We present a shape-conditioned intensity classifier (SCIC) and a dual-plan interface for DNN compiler fusion planning.
SCIC compares analytical arithmetic intensity with a datasheet Roofline ridge and recommends MemMode, ComputeMode, or both near that ridge.
The prototype binds these abstract plans to stock execution paths and separates analytical recommendation from latency calibration.
A correctness-gated evaluation covers 21 GEMM and nine attention shapes, optimized PyTorch/compiler/library baselines, recent fusion artifacts, and independently timed callable dispatch.
Auditing an earlier pilot exposed FP16 normalization overflow and motivated explicit FP32 statistics.
We integrate selection into a complete six-layer, 83.9-million-parameter random-weight Qwen3 architecture, preserving residual and FP16 rounding semantics.
Across six device/process repetitions on two A40 GPUs, SCIC's host-time ratios relative to the original compiled model are $0.870\times$ for prefill and $0.670\times$ for 64-step decode.
In actual vLLM 0.31.0 generation on one A40, its output-throughput ratio is $0.971\times$, with a process-bootstrap 95\% interval of $[0.961,0.978]$; all candidate request token hashes match the original engine.
These regressions show that eager-relative subgraph gains do not establish system acceleration.
Follow-up ablations locate integration costs; held-out projection rankings suggest local selection headroom, while measured model execution remains slower than the original.
The study contributes an analytical planning interface and auditable executable evidence, while leaving custom intensity-guided kernel benefits open.
Achieved DRAM intensity and pretrained task accuracy are not measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Operator fusion can reduce kernel launches and intermediate memory traffic in deep neural network execution.
Its performance also depends on the resources required by the fused computation: an epilogue may reduce data movement while changing the scheduling opportunities of its producer.
Consequently, deciding whether to fuse a producer--consumer pair requires more information than the identities and adjacency of the operators.
Tensor shape, data type, and the treatment of intermediates are relevant inputs to that decision.

The distinction between decoding and prefilling illustrates this dependence.
For a width-4096 FP16 projection, a cold off-chip traffic model gives approximately one FLOP per byte for a single decode row and 1024 FLOP/byte for 2048 prefill rows.
Attention adds an algorithmic dimension: an IO-aware prefill plan can increase its intensity with sequence length, whereas a plan that materializes scores approaches a fixed intensity ceiling.
These differences motivate a fusion interface that represents the resource regime of a concrete shape and can retain alternative plans near a predicted transition.

We propose a shape-conditioned intensity classifier (SCIC) and a dual-plan rule.
SCIC computes analytical arithmetic intensity and compares it with a hardware-specific datasheet Roofline ridge.
The rule produces MemMode for memory-oriented scheduling, ComputeMode for compute-oriented scheduling, or both within a configurable ridge band.
These modes describe intended scheduling choices, including epilogue placement and tile preference.
They are abstract plan templates. The original prototype evaluates stock-path bindings; the revised study in Section~\ref{sec:v2-method} tests five concrete projection schedules with an uncertainty-aware cost prior.

The prototype separates this analytical recommendation from executable path selection.
For MatMul+RMSNorm, MemMode is initially associated with a \texttt{torch.compile} chain and ComputeMode with an eager chain.
A latency probe can override that association within its configured scope: the pilot probes every GEMM shape, whereas the supplementary default probes ridge and compute regimes.
For attention, both candidate paths delegate the attention computation to PyTorch SDPA, and the executable policy compiles SDPA together with RMSNorm in every regime.
Thus the GEMM experiment evaluates calibrated selection, while the attention experiment evaluates a fixed library epilogue policy.
Neither experiment implements the custom tiled schedules represented by the abstract modes.

Numerical equivalence is also a prerequisite for interpreting path timings.
Auditing the original FP16 MatMul+RMSNorm implementation revealed overflow in the eager square operation on six of seven shapes, while the compiled path avoided those collapsed outputs.
We corrected the normalization to use explicit FP32 intermediates, validated complete outputs, and ran three new A40 processes.
These corrected pilot runs remain available for provenance; the main evaluation adds stronger baselines, actual dispatcher timing, and complete-model integration.

In the pilot primary corrected run, calibrated GEMM selection achieves $1.296\times$ geometric-mean speedup over eager, compared with $1.018\times$ for the analytical primary.
The probe matches 20 of 21 regular-timing choices across the three runs.
All probe choices compile the chain, and always-fuse achieves the same selected speedups, so this experiment does not establish a benefit over static compilation.
For attention, compiled and eager SDPA rankings depend on the shape and run.
The pilot evaluates recorded path rankings. The supplementary study measures executable selection against optimized static paths and validates a complete small-model adapter; its observed model slowdowns are reported directly.
Follow-up adapter ablations distinguish invocation overhead from complementary strict-projection candidates, with unprofiled timing and held-out ranking checks.
These earlier studies do not supply counter-based classification accuracy or custom tiled-schedule evidence. The revised study removes intensity-band candidate exclusions, prepares compiler-visible concrete schedules before outer compilation, and tests the analytical features against a matched no-AI ablation on held-out geometries.

\section{Background and problem formulation}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Roofline model and hardware anchors}
\label{sec:roofline}

The Roofline model relates arithmetic intensity to the balance between computational throughput and memory bandwidth~\cite{Williams2009Roofline}.
Arithmetic intensity $AI$ and the machine ridge $R$ are defined as

\begin{equation}
  AI = \frac{\mathrm{FLOPs}}{\mathrm{bytes}}, \qquad
  R = \frac{\mathrm{peak\ FLOP/s}}{\mathrm{peak\ byte/s}}.
  \label{eq:roofline}
\end{equation}

With peak compute throughput $P$ and peak bandwidth $B$, the classical model compares a compute-time estimate $\mathrm{FLOPs}/P$ with a transfer-time estimate $\mathrm{bytes}/B$ and takes the larger value.
An intensity below $R$ lies on the bandwidth-limited side of that model, while an intensity above $R$ lies on its compute-limited side.
This resource classification omits launch overhead, cache effects, and scheduling constraints.
We therefore use intensity as a planning feature rather than a predictor of the latency of an arbitrary compiled subgraph.

Table~\ref{tab:ridges} lists the datasheet anchors used by SCIC.
The compute peaks are dense Tensor-Core values without structural sparsity.
The A40 FP16 anchor is used for the executable evaluation; the other anchors support analytical comparisons only.
None of the listed peaks or ridges is an experimentally measured value from the evaluation device.

\subsection{Fusion planning and traffic assumptions}
\label{sec:modes}

We consider a producer followed by an epilogue, such as a matrix multiplication followed by RMSNorm.
The planning problem is to choose how the producer and epilogue share an execution region under the resource demands of a concrete shape.
MemMode represents a schedule that absorbs the epilogue into its producer, avoids an intermediate off-chip round trip, and prefers smaller tiles.
ComputeMode represents a schedule that keeps a heavy epilogue outside the matrix-multiply mainloop and prefers larger tiles.
For a GEMM-like producer, ComputeMode may materialize the product.
For attention, both modes retain the score computation within an IO-aware plan; they differ in tile preference and the placement of a further epilogue.

These descriptions define the plan vocabulary.
They do not imply that a particular compiler or library call implements the requested tile schedule.
The original prototype emits plan objects rather than custom kernels, and its executable bindings are specified separately in Section~\ref{sec:impl}. The concrete SCIC-v2 candidate pool is a separate, opt-in revision; it does not retrospectively change the original measurements.

The analytical traffic model assumes cold off-chip inputs and explicitly counted writes and rereads of materialized intermediates.
It excludes L2 reuse, causal masks, and paged KV caches, and counts a multiply-add as two FLOPs.
The resulting byte counts describe an idealized traffic model rather than an instruction trace or the traffic of a warmed benchmark.
The term off-chip memory is used throughout because the model also applies to the A40 evaluation device.

\section{Shape-conditioned classification and plan selection}
\label{sec:method}

The analytical method first estimates FLOPs and bytes for a site, then assigns a regime relative to the selected hardware ridge, and finally constructs one or two abstract plans.
Executable binding and latency calibration are separate steps described in Section~\ref{sec:impl}.
This separation allows a measured path choice to change without treating it as a new arithmetic-intensity measurement.

\subsection{GEMM intensity and epilogue traffic}
\label{sec:gemm-ai}

For $C{=}AB$ with shapes $[M,K]$, $[K,N]$, and $[M,N]$, let $e_A$, $e_B$, and $e_C$ denote element sizes in bytes.
Reading both inputs and writing the output once gives

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FLOPs} = 2MNK, \qquad
  \mathrm{bytes} = MK e_A + KN e_B + MN e_C.
  \label{eq:gemm}
\end{equation}

For a square projection $N{=}K{=}d$ with uniform element size $e$ ($e{=}2$ for FP16), the intensity simplifies to

\begin{equation}
  AI(M) = \frac{2Md}{(d + 2M)\,e}.
  \label{eq:square}
\end{equation}

Solving $AI(M){=}t$ for the row count yields

\begin{equation}
  M = \frac{t\, e\, d}{2(d - t\, e)} \quad (d > t\, e),
  \label{eq:mstar}
\end{equation}

Setting $t{=}R$ gives the balance row $M^{\star}$ in Table~\ref{tab:ridges}.
For the A40 FP16 anchor, $M^{\star}{=}240.3$; the row counts at $0.5R$ and $2R$ are $113.5$ and $544.6$.
Figure~\ref{fig:roofline} shows the corresponding datasheet roof and the analytical intensity curve.
For the seven MatMul+RMSNorm shapes, the logged producer intensities range from $1.00$ at $M{=}1$ to $1365$ at $M{=}4096$, passing through $120$ at $M{=}128$, $410$ at $M{=}512$, and $1024$ at $M{=}2048$.
The same projection can therefore receive different analytical regime labels during decoding and prefilling.

The producer intensity above is also the feature used to classify MatMul+RMSNorm; it is not the intensity of the complete chain.
To examine the potential traffic benefit of epilogue fusion separately, the unfused chain reads $A$ and $B$, writes and rereads $C$, reads the norm weight, and writes the normalized result.
An ideal fused schedule removes the write--read round trip of $C$, with only a small change in the FLOP count relative to GEMM.
For the square FP16 projection, the saved fraction of unfused bytes is approximately $2M/(d+4M)$ when the norm-weight term is neglected.
It increases from $0.05\%$ at $M{=}1$ and $1.5\%$ at $M{=}32$ to $10\%$ at $M{=}256$, $33\%$ at $M{=}2048$, and $40\%$ at $M{=}4096$.

Removing these bytes does not imply the same reduction in Roofline time.
With the A40 anchor and no launch term, the ratio of unfused to fused model times is $1.06\times$ at $M{=}128$ and $1.00\times$ at all tested shapes with $M \ge 512$, where both accountings are compute-limited.
Section~\ref{sec:gemm-eval} compares this idealized prediction with the existing PyTorch paths without assuming that the compiled chain realizes the ideal fused schedule.

\subsection{Attention prefill and decode}
\label{sec:attn-ai}

Let $Q,K,V$ have shape $[B,H,S,D]$.
For an IO-aware prefill plan, the leading FLOP count comes from the two matrix products.
Assuming that scores remain on chip and that $Q$, $K$, $V$, and the output each move once, the corresponding intensity is

\begin{equation}
  AI = S / e,
  \label{eq:fa}
\end{equation}

The head dimension cancels under these assumptions, and FP16 gives $AI{=}S/2$.
The evaluated sequence lengths 128, 512, and 2048 consequently have analytical intensities 64, 256, and 1024, which fall in the A40 memory, ridge, and compute regimes.
These are labels for the assumed IO-aware plan, not measurements of SDPA traffic.

Materializing the scores changes the asymptotic behavior.
Charging three memory passes over the score tensor gives an intensity ceiling of $4D/(3e)$ as $S$ grows.
For $D{=}128$ and FP16, that ceiling is $85.3$ FLOP/byte, below $0.5R$ for the H100 FP16 anchor ($R{=}295$).
Under this accounting, score materialization prevents the prefill plan from reaching the compute regime of that anchor.
The eager attention baseline explicitly materializes scores, whereas the SDPA paths delegate attention to the library.
A comparison between these paths therefore combines the attention implementation with epilogue placement.
The library backend and its realized traffic were not logged, so the analytical score-retention assumption is not independently validated by the benchmark.

Decode instead fixes the query length at $S_q{=}1$ and grows the KV cache.
For query and KV head counts $H_q$ and $H_{kv}$, the long-cache limit is

\begin{equation}
  AI \to \frac{2 H_q}{H_{kv}\, e}.
  \label{eq:decode}
\end{equation}

Multi-head attention in FP16 approaches $1$ FLOP/byte.
Grouped-query attention~\cite{Ainslie2023GQA} with $H_q/H_{kv}{=}8$ approaches $8$ FLOP/byte, still below $0.5R$ for every anchor in Table~\ref{tab:ridges}.
The two evaluated decode shapes use $H{=}8$ and $D{=}64$, with logged intensities $0.992$ and $1.000$.
They remain analytically memory-bound even as cache length increases.
Thus the compute-oriented prefill label should not be transferred to these decode workloads.

The analytical attention plans never set \texttt{materialize\_\allowbreak intermediate}.
Attention ComputeMode describes larger tiles and a further epilogue outside the mainloop; it preserves the IO-aware attention contract.

\subsection{Norms, reductions, and ragged grouped GEMM}
\label{sec:moe}

RMSNorm, LayerNorm, softmax, residual addition, and the other elementwise chains modeled by the prototype have $O(1)$ FLOP/byte in FP16.
They are assigned MemMode without a separate compute-oriented template.
For example, the model charges softmax on a materialized score matrix five FLOPs per score and three memory passes, giving intensity $5/(3e)$ independent of $S$.
These operation-count conventions are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:formulas}.

Ragged grouped GEMM illustrates why a layer-level aggregate may be insufficient for planning.
An expert with $t_e$ tokens computes $[t_e, H] \times [H, I]$.
Table~\ref{tab:moe} evaluates this computation for $H{=}4096$, $I{=}14336$, and FP16 against the H100 FP16 ridge.
Aggregate intensity is total FLOPs divided by total bytes across occupied experts, with inactive weights excluded except in the last row.
In the skewed case, the 8-token expert is memory-bound and the 1024-token expert is compute-bound, while the aggregate remains in the ridge band.
This analytical example motivates per-expert plan decisions rather than deriving a uniform template from the aggregate.
No mixture-of-experts layer is timed in the executable evaluation~\cite{Shazeer2017MoE}.

\subsection{Analytical classification and dual-plan rule}
\label{sec:selector}

SCIC uses a band around the ridge to limit sensitivity to small modeling errors near the strict Roofline balance point.
The default thresholds are $0.5R$ and $2R$.
These are configurable policy parameters rather than confidence bounds derived from measured uncertainty.
The strict bandwidth or compute resource is reported alongside the three-way regime.
Given the site report, analytical plan generation applies the following rules:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Elementwise chains, reductions, softmax, and norms produce MemMode only.
\item Other sites produce MemMode only if $AI < 0.5R$, and ComputeMode only if $AI > 2R$.
\item Sites within the ridge band produce both modes.
The primary is MemMode when $AI < R$ and ComputeMode otherwise; the other mode is retained as an alternative.
\item Attention plans preserve the IO-aware contract in either mode and never request score materialization.
\end{enumerate}

The output is a plan recommendation, not an executable kernel or an observed bottleneck.
In particular, the analytical rule alone offers no alternative outside the band.
Calibration deliberately relaxes that restriction when choosing between existing library and compiler paths: the pilot uses all-GEMM calibration, and the supplementary default uses ridge-plus-compute calibration.
Section~\ref{sec:scope} evaluates the consequences of limiting this calibration scope.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Resource modeling}
SCIC uses the classical Roofline formulation of Williams et al.~\cite{Williams2009Roofline} to produce a per-site planning feature.
The contribution is the use of shape-dependent traffic in the plan interface, rather than a new Roofline model.
Using datasheet values in place of measured throughput and bandwidth limits the interpretation of the resulting labels.

\paragraph{IO-aware attention}
FlashAttention and FlashAttention-2 organize attention around on-chip score processing to reduce memory traffic~\cite{Dao2022FlashAttention,Dao2023FlashAttention2}.
FlashAttention-3 further targets Hopper execution and low precision~\cite{Shah2024FlashAttention3}.
These systems implement attention kernels, while SCIC uses the distinction between IO-aware and score-materializing plans as an analytical input.
The pilot did not identify the selected SDPA backend. The supplementary artifact records available backend/kernel traces and measures a separate FlashAttention-2 path; names and traces alone do not measure its DRAM traffic.

\paragraph{Fusion and reduction scheduling}
ComFuse combines matrix multiplication with memory-intensive epilogue subgraphs and lowers schedules through a Stage-Stream model onto CUTLASS~\cite{Mu2026ComFuse,NVIDIACutlass}.
RedFuser generates fused forms for cascaded reductions, including attention-like patterns, using TVM~\cite{Tang2026RedFuser,Chen2018TVM}.
Neptune uses algebraic transformations of reduction dependences to recover locality and parallelism in attention and related GPU computations~\cite{Zhao2026Neptune}.
These approaches construct executable kernels or schedules.
SCIC produces analytical plan recommendations and selects executable paths. The supplementary study evaluates available research attention artifacts separately, including a rejected RedFuser regeneration; it does not claim to reproduce their published aggregate speedups.

\paragraph{Fusion granularity}
FuseFlow studies fusion granularity for sparse models on streaming dataflow hardware~\cite{Lacouture2026FuseFlow}.
SCIC addresses the resource regime of concrete dense-GPU shapes.
Both settings motivate choices among different execution regions, but the workloads and hardware evaluated here do not permit a direct performance comparison.

\paragraph{Compiler infrastructure}
TorchDynamo and TorchInductor provide the \texttt{torch.compile} execution paths in the prototype~\cite{Ansel2024PyTorch2}, with Triton as part of GPU code generation~\cite{Tillet2019Triton}.
Our harness compares their compiled chain with eager execution rather than modifying compiler fusion heuristics.
The observed disagreements with the analytical binding motivate testing how regime features and measured choices could inform those heuristics; the current experiment does not evaluate that integration.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\subsection{Interpretation of analytical labels}

The datasheet ridge is a reference value, and \texttt{measured\_\allowbreak ridge\_\allowbreak flops\_\allowbreak per\_\allowbreak byte} remains null.
A device-copy estimate in an earlier A40 package is marked \texttt{ESTIMATE\_\allowbreak COPY\_\allowbreak NOT\_\allowbreak NSIGHT} and does not measure that ridge.
The cold-byte model omits cache reuse, masks, paged KV access, and eager FP32 conversion temporaries, while the $0.5R$/$2R$ band and the softmax and RMSNorm operation counts are modeling choices.
No threshold sensitivity experiment establishes an optimal band.
The scope replay varies which recorded choices are used, not the accuracy of a measured resource classification.

The GEMM results expose a distinction between resource-oriented schedules and their executable proxies.
The MemMode/ComputeMode templates have not been tested as concrete tile sizes, register allocations, or shared-memory occupancies.
The all-GEMM ablation can select either existing path irrespective of the analytical regime.
Its measured outcomes evaluate candidate ranking and calibration coverage; they do not establish an incremental benefit of the analytical feature over empirical calibration.
The follow-up strict-projection candidates have complementary winning regions, but their held-out ranking gain excludes runtime dispatch and preparation costs (Section~\ref{sec:adapter-ablation}).
The grouped-GEMM examples and the H100/A100 anchors remain analytical results.

\subsection{Measurement scope}

No Nsight DRAM bytes were collected for the latency bakeoffs.
The earlier \texttt{ncu} attempt failed with \texttt{ERR\_NVGPUCTRPERM}.
A torch-profiler memory estimate from the separate stock-matmul archive equals the FP16 output-matrix size, causing its implied intensity to remain at $K{=}4096$ and classify every shape as compute-bound.
Agreement of that estimate with the analytical label on two of seven shapes is not a valid accuracy measurement.
The artifact therefore retains \texttt{quote\_\allowbreak as\_\allowbreak scic\_\allowbreak accuracy=false}.

The pilot selected latency copies a baseline measurement and excludes dispatch, switching, compilation, and calibration costs.
Its split-attention result sums separately warmed medians.
The supplementary executor instead measures its real calls and complete chains, records calibration cost, and inspects available backend/kernel traces.
Kernel names and launch counts do not substitute for isolated DRAM traffic or establish the causal benefit of fusion.
The earlier operator archive inspects kernels with a profiler before timing candidates in the same process.
The follow-up detects persistent post-profiler timing changes in its PyTorch 2.5.1 environment, so those earlier operator observations retain their instrumentation context and are not pooled with the new unprofiled matrix.
Every process in the final follow-up latency matrix excludes profiler initialization; separate diagnostic traces describe execution structure.
The original prototype emits no custom MemMode or ComputeMode kernels, no Stage-Stream schedule, and no FlashAttention-3 kernel. SCIC-v2 adds concrete Triton split-projection and cuBLAS/Triton epilogue candidates, without claiming that the abstract regime labels establish their optimality.
Fusion-proxy timings in the earlier A40 package are not used as fusion results here.
Complete random-weight small-model prefill, decode, and actual vLLM generation are measured; pretrained task accuracy, production request distributions, and cross-architecture model performance remain outside this evaluation.

\subsection{GPU conditions and generalization}
\label{sec:archives}

The corrected pilot consists of three processes on one A40 with seed-0 inputs.
The supplementary protocol expands shapes, software baselines, seeds, and device/process repetitions, but both GPUs are A40s.
The vLLM serving study uses three repetitions on one available A40 because the other device did not meet the idle gate.
The earlier small model uses random weights and controlled token lengths. The revised study additionally loads a pinned pretrained Qwen3-0.6B checkpoint; controlled inference and output-equivalence checks still do not establish task accuracy or generalization to production distributions.
Short probes can disagree with regular medians near a tie, as the pilot $M{=}128$ result shows.
Median timing and repetition do not supply a comprehensive uncertainty model.

Earlier archives remain available for provenance and are not pooled with the corrected results.
The original \texttt{bakeoff\_v4} and initial supplemental GEMM runs used the overflow-prone normalization; the earlier busy-GPU archives also differ in load and policy.
Their rankings cannot substantiate a validated fusion crossover or a causal contention effect.
The separate stock-matmul sweep does not use RMSNorm and is unaffected by this correction.

Further work should measure isolated DRAM traffic and device-specific resource limits, broaden numerically admitted schedule coverage, and evaluate the resulting kernels on production serving traces.
Attention calibration should include eager SDPA and consistently measured complete chains.
The current results do not demonstrate an advantage of calibrated GEMM selection over always compiling, or of abstract dual-plan generation over existing compiler heuristics.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

SCIC and the dual-plan interface express shape-dependent fusion recommendations from analytical traffic and a hardware-specific Roofline ridge.
Numerical equivalence, executable path selection, and complete-runtime performance are separate validation obligations.
The corrected pilot removes FP16 normalization overflow, but its eager-relative gains also occur under static compilation.
The supplementary evaluation measures real dispatch and stronger baselines rather than inferring selector performance from another path's timing.
In the complete random-weight small-model experiment, SCIC achieves $0.870\times$ prefill and $0.670\times$ decode speed ratios relative to the original compiled model, establishing a regression under the tested integration.
Actual vLLM generation likewise yields a $0.971\times$ throughput ratio, with a process-bootstrap interval of $[0.961,0.978]$, despite identical generated-token hashes.
Thus the current binding does not demonstrate aggregate system acceleration or a benefit over established compiler heuristics.
Unprofiled follow-up diagnostics partially recover integration losses through static strict inlining, reaching $0.973\times$ prefill and $0.897\times$ decode relative to their original-model control.
Known-shape, leave-one-seed-out projection selection yields $1.150\times$ host and $1.127\times$ event ratios over fixed compilation, excluding dispatch and calibration; this motivates testing calibration coverage and integration jointly rather than treating a local ranking gain as system acceleration.
The analytical interface remains a candidate planning feature; validating its kernel-generation value requires concrete alternative schedules and measurements of their realized resource use.

\subsection*{Pilot implementation and timing protocol}

\paragraph{Binding plans to PyTorch execution paths}

The recipes and timing accounting in this subsection describe the corrected pilot retained in Appendix~\ref{sec:pilot}.
Section~\ref{sec:executed} specifies the supplementary executor, its exact-signature cache, and independently measured calls.

\paragraph{MatMul plus RMSNorm}
The workload uses FP16 and $N{=}K{=}4096$ at seven row counts between $M{=}1$ and $M{=}4096$.
The eager baseline and \texttt{always\_unfuse} both execute the eager matrix product followed by RMSNorm, but are timed separately.
The default compile baseline and \texttt{always\_fuse} both use \texttt{torch.compile} on the chain, also with separate timing regions.
Their medians can differ despite sharing the same execution recipe.
The max-autotune baseline compiles the same graph with \texttt{mode="max-autotune"} when the call succeeds.
The analytical binding maps MemMode to \texttt{always\_fuse} and ComputeMode to \texttt{always\_unfuse}.
Both paths now compute RMSNorm statistics and weighting in FP32 and cast the final output back to FP16.
This precision specification is shared by the attention normalization helper.
These names identify benchmark paths; because the compiled graphs were not inspected, they do not prove a particular fusion pattern inside TorchInductor~\cite{Ansel2024PyTorch2}.

\paragraph{Attention plus RMSNorm}
The workload uses batch 1, eight heads, and head dimension 64.
Prefill lengths are 128, 512, and 2048; decode uses one query with cache lengths 128 and 2048.
The eager baseline explicitly forms the scores and probabilities in PyTorch before applying RMSNorm.
The \texttt{sdpa} baseline calls \texttt{scaled\_\allowbreak dot\_\allowbreak product\_\allowbreak attention} and then RMSNorm within one eager timing region.
\texttt{always\_fuse} compiles the SDPA-plus-RMSNorm chain.
\texttt{always\_unfuse} measures SDPA and RMSNorm separately and reports the sum of their two medians.
This sum is a split-region accounting, not a separately measured median of the complete sequential chain.
The SDPA backend was not logged, and the benchmark does not establish its score traffic or tile schedule.

The attention executable policy is \texttt{compile\_sdpa\_chain} in every regime, recorded as \texttt{always\_fuse}.
It therefore overrides epilogue placement when the analytical primary is ComputeMode with \texttt{fuse\_epilogue=false}.
The policy predates GEMM calibration and is evaluated as a fixed library binding rather than as the implementation of both analytical modes.

\paragraph{Pilot latency calibration and reported selection}

With calibration scope \texttt{all-gemm}, the harness probes both GEMM paths for one warmup and three timed iterations at every shape.
The path with the strictly smaller positive median becomes \texttt{measured\_winner}; a tie or missing measurement retains the analytical primary.
The winner can override the primary outside the ridge band as well as within it.
Attention is excluded from this probe.

The archive names this source \texttt{measured\_wallclock} and records \texttt{WALLCLOCK\_\allowbreak CALIBRATION\_\allowbreak NOT\_\allowbreak DRAM\_\allowbreak AI}.
On CUDA, the implementation measures elapsed GPU time with CUDA events, so we refer to it as latency calibration.
The legacy field names describe a timing-based choice and do not denote measured arithmetic intensity.

In the pilot harness, \texttt{scic\_selected} copies the regular-timing median of the baseline named by the decision log.
It is not a separately timed dispatcher.
For GEMM it follows the probe winner when available; for attention it copies the compiled chain.
Dispatch, plan-switch, compilation, and calibration setup costs are excluded from the selected-latency column.

The plan-cache key contains operator kind, dtype, machine, and a length bucket.
Buckets are $1$, $2$--$8$, $9$--$32$, $33$--$128$, $129$--$512$, $513$--$2048$, $2049$--$8192$, and $8193+$.
The GEMM harness appends the shape label when caching measured choices to avoid assuming that a single choice is valid for every shape in a bucket.
The experiment therefore does not validate reuse of one measured choice throughout a bucket.

\paragraph{Hardware, timing, and validation protocol}

The corrected pilot uses three independent processes on one selected NVIDIA A40, with PyTorch 2.5.1+cu121, CUDA 12.1, and Python 3.10.12.
The first process is designated the primary run before summarizing results; the other two assess repeatability.
Each process starts after two consecutive samples with utilization at most $5\%$ and used device memory at most $256$~MiB.
During-run GPU samples include the benchmark itself and cannot isolate other processes.
Dynamo's \texttt{cache\_size\_limit} is set to $64$.
The manifest stores exact commands, GPU samples, and a hash of the benchmark source.

Before timing, complete outputs are checked against reference computations at all twelve workload shapes.
For GEMM, the reference normalizes the FP16 matrix product using FP32 statistics; relative and absolute tolerances are both $10^{-3}$.
For attention, the reference performs attention and normalization with FP32 intermediates and casts the result to FP16; both tolerances are $10^{-2}$.
The numerical-validation record is tied to the benchmark-source hash, and the replication runner requires a passing record for that source.

Regular timing uses three warmups and ten iterations; the GEMM probe uses one warmup and three iterations.
CUDA events bracket the subgraph, and the device is synchronized after each iteration.
Compilation is completed before the timed region.
The probe is evaluated against the regular medians rather than against its own samples.
Max-autotune is intentionally skipped in all corrected runs.
Each run has 65 successful latency rows, 12 skipped max-autotune rows, and 12 decisions.
Nsight Compute is not invoked and achieved DRAM intensity remains unavailable.

The original \texttt{bakeoff\_v4} archive and the first supplemental legacy runs are retained separately.
Their GEMM ratios are not used as equivalent-computation speedups after the numerical discrepancy was identified.
The corrected pilot tables and figures use \path{paper/data/jsa_replication/run_01/}; repetitions and an offline calibration-scope replay are reported separately.


The evaluation first examines stock-GEMM shape dependence and the numerical validity of the compared chains, then assesses corrected path rankings and calibration scope.
The primary run supplies the main latency tables; all three corrected processes are summarized in Section~\ref{sec:replication}.
Speedup is the eager baseline median divided by the reported path latency; values below one indicate a slowdown.
Tables round recorded values, while geometric means use unrounded latency ratios.
The two-path minimum is the smaller regular-timing value of \texttt{always\_fuse} and \texttt{always\_unfuse}; selected gap is reported selected latency divided by that minimum.
This reference concerns available benchmark paths, not all possible kernels.

This replay establishes neither an optimal band nor the runtime cost advantage of a restricted search.
